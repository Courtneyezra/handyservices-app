The Handy Reception Desk

What we're building for customer messages, why, and what we learned by testing the idea on the last three months of real conversations. No technical words. Where one is unavoidable, it's explained at the bottom.

Ben is quick when he's there, about two minutes. The problem is the hours he isn't, and a set of half-built automations that either went quiet or sent things they shouldn't. We switched the risky one off on 2 September. Today the old assistant drafts replies and Ben taps approve on each one.

The idea: a reception desk with a fixed procedure

Think of every message, call, email or web chat arriving at one desk. The desk follows the same steps every time, and nothing can skip a step.

  1. One customer, one conversation
    Whether they call, WhatsApp, text or email, it all lands in the same thread. Ben sees one story per person.
  2. Sort it
    Is this a normal enquiry, or one of the things only Ben should touch: a price, a date, a complaint, a refund, an unusual job?
  3. Answer the easy part instantly
    A fixed, human-sounding message goes straight out: "Got it, can you send a photo and your postcode?" Any hour, within a minute.
  4. Prepare the hard part for Ben
    The system gathers photos, postcode and job details, drafts a reply or pre-fills a quote, and puts it in Ben's queue with a deadline.
  5. Check before anything sends
    Every outgoing message passes a checklist: no prices, no dates, no discounts, no promises, our voice only. Fail the checklist and it goes to Ben instead.
  6. Write it all down
    Every message, draft, approval and send is logged with who did it and why. If a customer ever asks "what happened?", we can answer.

The one rule that makes it safe: there is a single door out to customers, and it refuses any message that didn't come through the checklist. Today there are sixteen doors. That's how 31 August happened.

Complaints skip every automated role. Ben's phone buzzes immediately. The system does not apologise in a way that admits fault, does not offer a refund, and does not promise a date. It keeps the customer warm and gets a human on it.

What we tested on real conversations

We took every real customer message from June to September, 132 conversations in all, and ran them through the desk's rules on paper. This is what would have happened.

Messages with no reply of any kind in a day23%0%0%

Three things stood out. First, silence disappears on day one, and it's fixed wording that does it, not AI. Second, Ben's workload doesn't drop straight away. It changes from writing to approving, and that approving is what teaches the system what "good" looks like. By month two it halves. Third, the messages that still wait are almost all price and date questions, which wait for Ben on purpose. So Ben's hours decide the customer's experience more than any technology.

One honest miss. The paper test used simple keyword rules to sort messages, and it wrongly sent "I need a curtain pole fitted near NG4" to Ben's lane as a money question. That's why the real sorter takes one quick AI look at each message instead of just matching words.

Two caveats. WhatsApp wasn't being recorded properly until mid-August, so most of the test data is from the last three weeks. And Ben's replies from his personal phone aren't in the records, so some "unanswered" messages were probably answered by him from there.

What it costs

About 8p a day in AI costs at today's volume, and under £4 a month even at double the traffic. Message fees to WhatsApp and the text network are about the same again. Against that, the desk gives back roughly 20 minutes of Ben's day at launch and over an hour by month two. Opening a second city is a settings change, not a second bill.

What happens next

  1. Close the doors
    Make the single door out the only way to reach a customer. Stop laptops being able to send. Connect Ben's personal WhatsApp so the desk knows when he's already replied. Type in the contractors' phone numbers. A couple of days.
  2. See everything, never go silent
    Turn on the log. Give Ben approve, edit and reject buttons with a reason. Switch on the receptionist and the 20-minute holding line. About three days.
  3. Build the desk
    The sorter, the scoper, the quote clerk, the checker, running alongside the old assistant so we can compare before switching. Two to three weeks.
  4. Let it earn trust
    Reply types graduate to automatic when the tests pass and Ben has approved enough of them unedited. Mostly waiting. About two weeks.
  5. Widen
    Video understanding, the quote card inside the chat, contractor messaging, email and web chat as full channels.
  6. Clean up
    Delete the old assistants.

Decisions we need from you

  • Ben's hours. When is he "on"? Is tapping approvals paid time? The customer's experience follows this more than anything else.
  • Should automatic messages say they're automatic? A brand call. Today they sound like a person.
  • How long before the holding line? The model assumed 20 minutes. Shorter feels attentive, longer gives Ben more room.
  • What "good" means at 30 days. Fewer unanswered messages, fewer taps for Ben, more deposits, or faster first reply. Pick the one we judge it by.

Words we use

Burst
A run of messages from one customer within ten minutes. Counted as one thing to answer.
Lane
Where a message is sent after sorting: the receptionist, the scoper, Ben, or the bin.
Holding line
A fixed message that says we've seen it and someone's on it. Sent when nobody has replied in 20 minutes.
Draft
A reply the AI wrote that Ben must approve before it goes.
Flag
A message only Ben should handle, with a deadline attached.
Earning it
A reply type moves from "Ben approves" to "sends itself" only after tests pass and Ben has approved enough of them without editing. It moves back if it gets one wrong.
The door
The one piece of code allowed to send a message to a customer. It refuses anything that skipped the checklist.
